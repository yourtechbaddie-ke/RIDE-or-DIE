# RIDE OR DIE — World Visual Realism Bible

## Production Status
Visual direction upgraded for the realistic-universe production phase.

## Non-negotiable visual standard
RIDE OR DIE must never look like a collection of generic game environments, stock apocalypse backgrounds, repeated NPC templates or decorative AI scenery.

Every location must feel inhabited by people who existed there before the player arrived.

The target is **cinematic realism with lived-in imperfection**: believable architecture, physically plausible materials, natural human scale, environmental wear, regional variation, functional objects and evidence of routines that continue independently of the player.

## World-building rules

### 1. Every building has a reason to exist
Homes, shops, clinics, schools, offices, garages, warehouses, restaurants and public spaces must communicate their purpose through layout, furniture, signage, equipment and wear.

### 2. No sterile surfaces
Use realistic variation in dust, fingerprints, scuffs, water marks, faded paint, chipped edges, repaired surfaces, clutter and maintenance quality. Do not apply uniform grunge to everything.

### 3. Human-scale detail
World imagery should include believable small-scale details: discarded packaging, personal photographs, half-finished meals, laundry, children's objects, receipts, tools, chargers, medication containers, notebooks, keys, bags, traffic notices and improvised repairs where appropriate.

### 4. Socioeconomic realism
Not every street, home or vehicle should have the same quality. Wealth, occupation, age, family structure and local economy should visibly influence spaces and possessions.

### 5. Weather affects the world
Rain changes surfaces. Heat changes vegetation and human behavior. Cold changes clothing and condensation. Wind moves loose materials. Weather must interact with architecture, vehicles, vegetation and people rather than appearing as a visual filter.

### 6. Population density is contextual
Busy places should contain overlapping human activity. Quiet places should still show evidence that people live, work or previously passed through them.

### 7. Vehicles are personal objects
Vehicles require different cleanliness levels, damage, accessories, cargo, maintenance states, stickers, seat arrangements and ownership clues.

### 8. Vegetation is ecological
Plants should respond to climate, water access, human maintenance and abandonment. Do not scatter identical plants across unrelated regions.

## The eight major regions

### The Last City
Recognizable modern urban life before and during early Collapse. Mixed residential and commercial districts, traffic infrastructure, public transport, offices, restaurants, schools, apartment blocks, service roads and ordinary domestic interiors. The transition should begin subtly: shortages, queues, emergency notices, abnormal traffic patterns and increasingly anxious behavior.

### The Black Highway
A functioning road network gradually becoming dangerous. Fuel stations, service areas, road barriers, abandoned vehicles, roadside businesses, repair points, tire marks, crash evidence, temporary camps and changing traffic patterns should tell the story of travel becoming survival.

### Hollowgate
A transitional settlement built around adaptation rather than fantasy-apocalypse aesthetics. Existing structures are repurposed. People create routines, rules, trading points, sleeping areas, repair spaces, kitchens, watch positions and private corners. The settlement should feel socially inhabited rather than procedurally populated.

### The Verdant
A biologically aggressive but believable landscape where vegetation increasingly overtakes infrastructure. Show roots damaging roads, moisture affecting walls, wildlife traces, improvised paths and structures slowly disappearing beneath growth. Beauty and danger coexist.

### The Ashen Expanse
A disaster-shaped environment with altered visibility, heat, particulate residue, damaged infrastructure and scarce resources. Avoid a monochrome wasteland. Materials retain distinct physical identities and survivors adapt differently depending on where they came from.

### The Drowned
Flooded infrastructure creates a new geography. Water levels vary, routes become inaccessible, buildings show different flood heights, vehicles become stranded, mold and water damage appear selectively, and survivors construct practical elevated routes and temporary crossings.

### The Silent North
Cold isolation changes architecture, clothing, movement, lighting, acoustics and resource use. Frost, snow load, condensation, frozen surfaces, enclosed interiors and long periods of low human activity create a different survival rhythm.

### The Origin
The most controlled and unsettling region. It should initially appear cleaner, more deliberate and better preserved than surrounding areas. Its visual language should contain subtle continuity clues connecting earlier locations without making the mystery obvious.

## Human presence rules
NPCs must not stand in place waiting for the player.

Their appearance, posture and activity should correspond to context: workers carry work-related objects, parents monitor children, tired people sit differently, injured people compensate for pain, strangers maintain personal distance, friends interrupt one another and people sometimes ignore the player completely.

Crowds must contain independent micro-behaviors: conversations, phone use, eating, waiting, searching, carrying objects, checking doors, looking at traffic, helping someone, arguing and leaving.

## Environmental storytelling
Every major scene should answer at least one question without dialogue:

- Who was here?
- What were they doing?
- What changed?
- What are they trying to preserve?
- What did they abandon?
- Who owns this object?
- What happened shortly before the player arrived?

## Image-generation / asset brief
When generating replacement world images, use:

**photorealistic cinematic 3D game environment, physically based materials, realistic global illumination, natural human scale, believable architecture, lived-in details, imperfect surfaces, contextual clutter, realistic weather interaction, documentary-level environmental storytelling, subtle color grading, high-end AAA survival game quality, no fantasy game UI, no generic apocalypse template, no repeated assets, no plastic surfaces, no mannequin-like people, no empty decorative streets.**

People visible in world imagery must have varied faces, body types, clothing, posture and behavior. They must look like ordinary people caught in an unfolding crisis, not posed models.

## Quality gate
A world image fails production if it feels:

- generic
- empty
- staged
- too clean
- uniformly destroyed
- populated by mannequin-like NPCs
- visually repetitive
- disconnected from local architecture or climate
- designed only to look impressive rather than inhabited

The correct question is not **"Does this look like a game?"**

It is:

> **"Could I believe that thousands of people lived here before I arrived—and that their lives continued after I left?"**
