# Chapter 3 — Layer 47: Character Routine & Life Simulation

## Status
**Canon direction locked.**

## Purpose
Make RIDE OR DIE's characters feel like people with lives rather than NPCs waiting for the player.

## Core principle
> **Characters do not exist only when the camera is watching them.**

Before The Collapse, characters have routines, obligations, habits, preferences, relationships and imperfect days. After The Collapse, those routines are disrupted, adapted or abandoned according to circumstances.

## 1. Living routines

Characters can have context-sensitive routines such as:
- waking;
- getting dressed;
- showering;
- preparing food;
- eating;
- cleaning;
- working;
- attending school;
- shopping;
- commuting;
- exercising;
- watching television;
- watching a movie;
- talking with family;
- calling friends;
- sleeping.

Routines should be authored around personality rather than copied identically between characters.

## 2. Routines are not chores

The player is not required to complete every domestic action.

Routine interactions exist to:
- reveal character;
- create immersion;
- establish locations;
- introduce relationships;
- teach later survival mechanics;
- create optional moments of discovery.

## 3. Character-specific habits

A character might:
- wake early;
- sleep late;
- skip breakfast;
- cook elaborate meals;
- leave dishes everywhere;
- keep an immaculate home;
- watch a favourite programme;
- check the news obsessively;
- exercise every morning;
- spend too much time on their phone;
- prepare meals for a child;
- avoid certain people.

Habits should communicate personality without requiring exposition.

## 4. Household dynamics

Households should have believable shared routines.

Examples:
- parents preparing children for school;
- partners cooking together;
- siblings arguing over television;
- roommates sharing chores;
- grandparents visiting;
- children refusing breakfast;
- someone leaving for work while another is still asleep.

The player can sometimes enter these moments rather than triggering them manually.

## 5. Autonomous NPC behaviour

Background characters may perform routines without direct player control.

The simulation can use lightweight scheduling rather than fully simulating every person at every second.

The goal is perceived continuity and meaningful persistence, not unnecessary computational cost.

## 6. Routine interruption

Events can interrupt ordinary routines.

Examples:
- a phone call;
- a visitor;
- a late delivery;
- a work emergency;
- a child's problem;
- a traffic incident;
- a news alert;
- a power outage;
- an early Collapse warning.

The interruption can create organic story opportunities.

## 7. Pre-Collapse media

Television, radio and other media can be watched or heard naturally.

They may contain:
- entertainment;
- advertisements;
- weather;
- celebrity news;
- public announcements;
- early reports about anomalies.

A player who is paying attention may notice information that another player ignores.

## 8. Cooking as character expression

Cooking is not merely a hunger mechanic.

Recipes, preparation style, timing and who receives the meal can reveal:
- culture;
- personality;
- family relationships;
- wealth;
- stress;
- affection;
- routine.

Later, the same system can become a survival mechanic.

## 9. Eating and social moments

Meals can become natural relationship scenes.

Characters may:
- eat together;
- argue during meals;
- celebrate good news;
- hide information;
- invite someone unexpectedly;
- eat alone after an argument.

These scenes should be playable or interruptible where appropriate.

## 10. Sleeping and waking

Sleep is part of the world state.

Before The Collapse, sleep can mostly establish routine.

After The Collapse, sleep becomes strategically important because:
- exhaustion affects performance;
- shelter safety matters;
- night travel may be dangerous;
- someone may need to stay awake to guard others;
- injuries may require rest.

## 11. Character work lives

Occupations should influence routines.

A chef, nurse, teacher, driver, executive, performer, mechanic or stay-at-home parent should not share the same daily schedule.

Occupational identity should be visible through:
- clothing;
- equipment;
- locations;
- communications;
- time constraints;
- relationships.

## 12. Children and family routines

Children should have their own rhythms and needs.

These can include:
- school;
- play;
- meals;
- sleep;
- learning;
- attachment to caregivers.

Tariq's needs evolve with age, while his parents and other caregivers age alongside him.

## 13. Routine-to-survival transformation

The same character behaviour should acquire new meaning after The Collapse.

Examples:

**Morning shower** → water conservation.

**Breakfast** → rationing.

**Commuting** → route planning.

**Shopping** → scavenging.

**Television** → searching for surviving information.

**Sleeping** → securing shelter.

**Cooking** → managing scarce ingredients and fuel.

**Cleaning** → sanitation and disease prevention.

This creates mechanical continuity between the peaceful world and survival world.

## 14. Character absence

If a character is elsewhere, their home or usual location should not always become an empty game stage.

The player may encounter:
- evidence of recent activity;
- a meal left unfinished;
- a television still running;
- a message;
- a missing item;
- a note;
- a changed routine.

Absence can tell a story.

## 15. Relationship overlap

A character's routine can intersect with another character's routine.

This allows the player to discover relationships organically.

Examples:
- two people repeatedly meeting at a café;
- a parent collecting a child;
- coworkers sharing a commute;
- a secret relationship visible through unusual schedule overlaps.

## 16. Player observation

The player can learn about people simply by watching how they live.

This supports the game's emphasis on discovery and emotional attachment.

The player should sometimes think:

> “I know this person because I've seen how they actually live.”

## 17. Production rule

Not every routine needs bespoke animation or simulation.

High-value routines receive the greatest detail.

Background routines can use:
- reusable animation systems;
- schedule states;
- contextual interactions;
- environmental storytelling.

The design target is **believable life**, not exhaustive simulation.

## 18. Genre pacing safeguard

Routine content should remain compact and skippable when appropriate.

The game must transition from ordinary life into genuine survival pressure early enough to retain the intended audience.

## 19. Canonical design statement

> **Before The Collapse, we show people living. After The Collapse, we show what happens when living becomes surviving.**

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Characters have individualized routines.
- [ ] Routine actions can be physically interacted with.
- [ ] Domestic life feels lived-in.
- [ ] Households behave believably.
- [ ] Background NPCs can have autonomous routines.
- [ ] Routine interruptions create organic events.
- [ ] Media can contain early world signals.
- [ ] Cooking and meals contribute to characterization.
- [ ] Occupations affect schedules and environments.
- [ ] Children have age-appropriate routines.
- [ ] Tariq's routine changes with age.
- [ ] Pre-Collapse routines transform into survival mechanics.
- [ ] Absence can communicate narrative information.
- [ ] Relationships can be discovered through routine overlap.
- [ ] Routine systems do not turn the game into a chore simulator.
- [ ] Survival pacing remains clear.

## Direction lock
**Make them live. Then make the player fight to keep that life alive.**
