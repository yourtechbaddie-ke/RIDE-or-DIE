# Chapter 2 — Layer 36: Time Passage & Living World Calendar

## Status
**Creative direction locked.**

## Purpose
Make time itself a gameplay system. Ride or Die should feel like a world that continues moving before, between and after player actions.

This layer connects the autonomous scene engine, world state, relationships, events, family continuity and environmental persistence.

---

# 1. Core principle

> **Time does not stop because the player is looking somewhere else.**

Days should have rhythms. People should have schedules. Events should have dates. Some opportunities should disappear. Other opportunities should appear because time passed.

---

# 2. World calendar

The game maintains a persistent calendar containing:

- day;
- date;
- time of day;
- week progression;
- seasons where appropriate;
- public/social events;
- birthdays;
- weddings;
- births;
- burials;
- appointments;
- work shifts;
- school schedules;
- travel plans;
- deadlines;
- temporary opportunities.

---

# 3. Time-of-day states

Scenes and behaviour should respond to:

- dawn;
- morning;
- midday;
- afternoon;
- evening;
- night;
- late night.

These are not merely lighting presets.

They affect who is awake, where people are, what businesses are open, what sounds exist and what risks become plausible.

---

# 4. Character routines

Characters can have recurring routines based on their occupations, responsibilities and personalities.

Examples:
- a school bus driver starts early;
- a CEO has meetings and private commitments;
- a parent prepares a child for sleep;
- a vendor closes a stall;
- a nun follows religious obligations;
- a musician performs or travels;
- a teenager attends school or meets friends.

Routines can be disrupted by story events.

---

# 5. Routine disruption

Major events can break established routines.

Examples:
- injury prevents work;
- pregnancy changes mobility and schedule;
- a newborn changes sleep;
- grief causes someone to miss work;
- a death leaves a household without its usual caregiver;
- a secret meeting creates an unusual late-night absence.

The player can notice these changes without a notification telling them why.

---

# 6. Scheduled events

Major social events are anchored to the calendar.

Supported events include:
- weddings;
- birthdays;
- births;
- funerals/burials;
- religious ceremonies;
- concerts;
- school events;
- workplace gatherings;
- private meetings.

Events can be prepared for before they occur and leave physical and emotional residue afterward.

---

# 7. Event preparation

The world should visibly prepare for important events.

A wedding can produce:
- invitations;
- clothing preparation;
- food preparation;
- decorations;
- guest travel;
- private conversations;
- last-minute conflict.

A birthday can produce:
- shopping;
- gifts;
- cake preparation;
- decorations;
- guest planning.

The player can encounter these preparations before the main event.

---

# 8. Event aftermath

Events remain part of the world after they end.

Examples:
- wedding flowers remain temporarily;
- photographs are taken;
- gifts enter inventories/homes;
- guests remember who attended;
- a birthday changes a child's age and memories;
- a burial creates a grave and grief state.

---

# 9. Missed opportunities

Some opportunities are time-sensitive.

If the player does not act, an event may:
- happen without them;
- become inaccessible;
- change form;
- be postponed;
- be interrupted by another event;
- produce a different consequence.

The game should never imply that every meaningful event waits indefinitely.

---

# 10. Delayed discovery

Time can create mystery.

The player may discover something weeks after it occurred.

Examples:
- a photograph taken earlier;
- a diary entry written before death;
- an object moved from its original location;
- a relationship that developed privately;
- evidence of a secret meeting.

The player must sometimes reconstruct chronology.

---

# 11. Aging and life progression

Where the story's timeframe permits, characters can age naturally.

Age progression can affect:
- appearance;
- responsibilities;
- relationships;
- routines;
- family roles;
- social expectations.

Do not age characters merely for spectacle; use it when meaningful to the narrative.

---

# 12. Pregnancy and birth timeline

Pregnancy should be represented as a progression rather than a single event.

Where narratively appropriate, changes can include:
- appointments;
- fatigue;
- preparation;
- household changes;
- mobility changes;
- anticipation;
- birth;
- postpartum recovery;
- infant care.

The timeline should remain coherent and respectful.

---

# 13. Child development continuity

Children should not remain frozen in one state indefinitely when the narrative timeframe supports progression.

Age-appropriate changes can affect:
- routines;
- speech;
- movement;
- interests;
- relationships;
- memories.

The game should only simulate progression at the granularity required by the story.

---

# 14. Weather and temporal atmosphere

Weather can reinforce time passage and world continuity.

Examples:
- rain interrupts outdoor plans;
- heat changes routines;
- storms affect travel;
- morning light enters rooms differently;
- nighttime changes ambient sound.

Weather should influence scenes where plausible rather than functioning only as visual decoration.

---

# 15. Travel and absence

Characters may travel for:
- work;
- family;
- school;
- performances;
- escape;
- secret meetings;
- emergencies.

Their absence can create social and narrative consequences.

The protagonist can also travel, causing other characters to continue without them.

---

# 16. Calendar-based character memory

Characters can remember dates associated with important events.

Examples:
- a birthday;
- anniversary;
- death date;
- wedding date;
- date of betrayal;
- date a child was born.

A date can later trigger behaviour, grief or reflection.

---

# 17. Recurring events

Some events can recur annually or periodically.

Recurring events can change because of previous world state.

Example:

A family celebrates a birthday one year with a full gathering.

After a death, the next celebration may be smaller, tense or cancelled.

The player experiences the difference without needing an exposition screen.

---

# 18. Time skips

Long narrative transitions may use time skips when appropriate.

A time skip must preserve:
- major world-state consequences;
- relationship progression;
- injuries;
- births;
- deaths;
- environmental changes;
- important unresolved secrets.

The player should understand enough of what changed without requiring every intervening day to be playable.

---

# 19. Pacing protection

Realistic time progression must not become tedious.

Use narrative compression for:
- repetitive travel;
- routine work;
- ordinary sleep;
- unimportant waiting.

Show detailed time passage when it affects character emotion, discovery or consequence.

---

# 20. Layer 36 acceptance criteria

- [ ] The game maintains a persistent calendar.
- [ ] Time of day affects behaviour and environment.
- [ ] Characters have plausible routines.
- [ ] Major events can disrupt routines.
- [ ] Weddings, birthdays, births and burials are calendar events.
- [ ] Event preparation is visible before major events.
- [ ] Events leave aftermath after completion.
- [ ] Time-sensitive opportunities can be missed.
- [ ] Delayed discoveries can reveal earlier events.
- [ ] Character aging is supported where narratively appropriate.
- [ ] Pregnancy and birth follow a coherent timeline.
- [ ] Child progression can persist across the story timeframe.
- [ ] Weather can affect time and activity.
- [ ] Travel creates real absences.
- [ ] Important dates can become emotional memory anchors.
- [ ] Recurring events can change based on world history.
- [ ] Time skips preserve causal continuity.
- [ ] Narrative compression prevents realism from becoming tedious.

## Direction lock
**Time is not a loading screen between chapters. It is one of the forces changing the people, places and relationships inside Ride or Die.**
