# Chapter 2 — Layer 30: Environmental Realism & Lived-In World

## Status
**Creative direction locked.**

## Purpose
Translate the cinematic-realism philosophy into the physical world. Locations should feel inhabited, used, altered by weather, shaped by people and capable of retaining traces of what happened there.

---

# 1. Core principle

> **The environment remembers people even when people are gone.**

A room should communicate history without requiring exposition.

Examples:
- an unfinished meal;
- a child's drawing;
- a repaired chair;
- photographs moved from one wall to another;
- worn floor paths;
- a pot left on a cold stove;
- a recently disturbed bed;
- a grave behind a building;
- decorations from an event that already happened.

---

# 2. Environmental storytelling

Every important location should answer some combination of:

- Who lives here?
- Who used to live here?
- What do they value?
- What happened recently?
- What are they hiding?
- What has changed?
- What evidence has been left behind?

Avoid making every environmental detail a clue. Some objects exist simply because people live there.

---

# 3. Domestic realism

Homes and private spaces should contain believable evidence of routine.

Potential details:
- dishes;
- laundry;
- toiletries;
- books;
- children's toys;
- medicine;
- food storage;
- photographs;
- personal clothing;
- damaged appliances;
- handwritten notes;
- unfinished projects;
- sentimental objects.

Object placement should reflect character personality and household relationships.

---

# 4. Cooking and food

Cooking is an environmental and social activity rather than a resource-management mini-game by default.

The world can represent:
- ingredients;
- preparation;
- cookware;
- heat;
- steam;
- smell cues;
- serving;
- portions;
- leftovers;
- spoiled food;
- shared meals.

Food can become narrative evidence.

Examples:
- a meal prepared for someone who never arrives;
- a birthday cake left untouched;
- two plates when only one person claims to have eaten;
- a pot still warm after everyone has supposedly left.

---

# 5. Weather and time

Weather and time should alter the emotional character of locations.

Systems can influence:
- visibility;
- reflections;
- sound propagation;
- wet surfaces;
- mud;
- clothing;
- fire;
- travel;
- NPC routines;
- gathering behaviour;
- indoor/outdoor activity.

Day/night transitions should support believable routines rather than simply change lighting presets.

---

# 6. Persistent event remnants

Major events leave traces.

### Wedding
Afterward, the player may discover:
- flowers;
- decorations;
- food containers;
- photographs;
- discarded clothing accessories;
- handwritten vows;
- gifts;
- changed relationships.

### Birthday
Afterward:
- balloons or decorations;
- cake remnants;
- wrapping paper;
- drawings;
- photographs;
- a new gift in a child's room.

### Burial
Afterward:
- disturbed earth;
- flowers;
- personal objects;
- footprints;
- a makeshift marker;
- someone visiting the grave.

The remnants should disappear or change naturally over time.

---

# 7. Locations as emotional spaces

The same location can feel different depending on what happened there.

A kitchen can be:
- warm during a family meal;
- tense during an argument;
- terrifying after a violent event;
- painfully quiet after someone's death.

Reuse important spaces so the player develops spatial memory.

---

# 8. Environmental sound

Spaces should contain layered sound appropriate to their condition.

Examples:
- refrigerator hum;
- plumbing;
- distant voices;
- footsteps above;
- wind through damaged windows;
- rain on roofs;
- utensils;
- generators;
- insects;
- birds;
- traffic remnants;
- children playing;
- silence where sound previously existed.

A missing sound can itself communicate a change.

---

# 9. Evidence without exposition

Environmental evidence can contradict spoken testimony.

A character may claim:
> "Nobody has been here."

But the player notices:
- fresh food;
- a recently moved chair;
- wet footprints;
- a warm cup;
- a recently written note.

The game should trust player observation.

---

# 10. Personal spaces and the paparazzi system

The Personal Paparazzi System from Layer 29 can use environmental realism to make discoveries credible.

The protagonist's belongings should have stable locations and believable access patterns.

A photograph appearing in a jacket should matter because the player knows:
- when the jacket was worn;
- where it was left;
- who had access to it;
- whether the protagonist was alone.

The environment becomes evidence of impossible proximity.

---

# 11. Emotional object persistence

Objects connected to deceased characters should not immediately disappear.

Possible states:
- untouched;
- packed away;
- redistributed;
- destroyed;
- preserved by a loved one;
- placed at a grave;
- discovered by the player.

A simple object can become emotionally significant because the player remembers who owned it.

---

# 12. Character-driven environmental change

NPCs should alter spaces according to their lives.

Examples:
- a new mother reorganizes a room around a baby;
- a grieving person stops maintaining a space;
- a child adds drawings to a wall;
- a couple moves belongings together after marriage;
- someone preparing to leave begins packing;
- someone hiding something creates a false sense of normality.

These changes can occur while the player is elsewhere.

---

# 13. Cinematic staging

Environmental composition should support camera language without feeling like a stage set.

Use:
- practical light sources;
- depth through doorways and windows;
- foreground obstruction;
- reflections;
- believable clutter;
- natural camera height;
- motivated movement.

Do not overdecorate every scene. Real spaces have empty, boring and imperfect areas.

---

# 14. Realism hierarchy

When production resources are limited, prioritize realism in this order:

1. Story-critical locations.
2. Spaces where characters routinely live.
3. Locations containing major discoveries.
4. Major event spaces.
5. Frequently traversed public areas.
6. Transitional spaces.
7. Background spaces.

The goal is not maximum detail everywhere. It is **maximum believability where the player pays attention**.

---

# 15. Player freedom

The player should be able to inspect meaningful environmental details without turning every object into a collectible.

Interactions should be selective and contextual.

Examples:
- open a diary;
- inspect a photograph;
- smell or examine food where appropriate;
- sit at a table;
- touch a keepsake;
- examine a grave;
- notice a changed room;
- listen through a door.

---

# 16. Layer 30 acceptance criteria

- [ ] Locations communicate character history.
- [ ] Domestic spaces feel lived in.
- [ ] Cooking and food support realism and storytelling.
- [ ] Weather/time affect atmosphere and routines.
- [ ] Weddings, birthdays and burials leave persistent traces.
- [ ] Important spaces change after major events.
- [ ] Environmental audio communicates presence and absence.
- [ ] Objects can retain emotional significance after death.
- [ ] NPC actions can alter environments without player presence.
- [ ] Paparazzi discoveries remain physically believable.
- [ ] Environmental clues can contradict dialogue.
- [ ] Cinematic staging remains naturalistic.
- [ ] Detail is prioritized according to narrative importance.

## Direction lock
**Ride or Die's world should feel lived in before the player arrives, altered while the player is present, and changed after the player leaves.**
