# Chapter 2 — Layer 39: Named Character Canon & Life Identity

## Status
**Creative direction locked.**

## Purpose
Make every meaningful person in RIDE OR DIE an identifiable human being with a name, history, relationships and continuity. Generic identifiers such as “Character 014” are not part of the player-facing experience.

---

# 1. Core principle

> **Nobody important is just a number.**

Every named character should feel like someone who existed before the player met them and whose life matters after the player leaves.

---

# 2. Names are canonical

Every major and supporting character receives a canonical name before their production identity is finalized.

Names should be appropriate to:
- character background;
- family context;
- geography;
- generation;
- culture;
- personal history.

Placeholder IDs may exist internally for databases, but they must never replace the character's name in narrative presentation.

---

# 3. Newborns have names

A newborn baby is a real character in the world and receives a canonical name.

If a baby is born during the game, the naming event can itself become part of the story.

Possible systems include:
- parents choosing the name;
- family disagreement;
- honoring a deceased relative;
- cultural naming traditions;
- a meaningful middle name;
- a name discovered later through family records.

The newborn should not be referred to indefinitely as “the baby.”

---

# 4. Character identity record

Every named character should have a canonical identity record containing, where relevant:

- full name;
- preferred name;
- age/date of birth;
- occupation or role;
- family relationships;
- important relationships;
- home/location;
- personal history;
- personality traits;
- motivations;
- fears;
- values;
- secrets;
- routines;
- wardrobe identity;
- visual reference;
- voice identity;
- important possessions;
- significant memories;
- current world-state status.

Not every field must be exposed to the player.

---

# 5. Naming philosophy

Names should feel natural rather than algorithmically decorative.

Avoid:
- excessive repetition;
- names chosen only because they sound dramatic;
- stereotypes based on culture;
- forced uniqueness at the expense of realism.

Ordinary names are welcome.

A person's emotional importance comes from who they are and what happens to them—not from having an unusual name.

---

# 6. Names can carry history

A name can become emotionally significant through repeated story exposure.

Examples:
- a parent's nickname for their child;
- a spouse's private name for a partner;
- a childhood nickname;
- a professional stage name;
- a name someone stopped using;
- a name changed after marriage or another life event.

Different characters may call the same person by different names depending on their relationship.

---

# 7. Public and private identities

Some characters may have multiple legitimate identities or names.

Examples:
- celebrity stage name versus legal name;
- professional title versus private name;
- married versus birth surname;
- alias used for safety;
- childhood nickname.

This must be grounded in the character's story.

---

# 8. Character recognition

The game should reinforce recognition through multiple channels:
- name;
- face;
- voice;
- mannerisms;
- clothing;
- relationships;
- location;
- possessions;
- recurring routines.

The player should be able to recognize a character even before a nameplate appears.

---

# 9. Children and identity continuity

Children receive the same continuity treatment appropriate to their developmental stage.

A child can have:
- a name;
- caregivers;
- siblings;
- routines;
- preferences;
- memories;
- fears;
- attachments.

Infants have fewer expressed traits, but they still have an identity and family context.

---

# 10. Birth as identity creation

A birth event can create a new named world entity.

The birth can update:
- family relationships;
- household composition;
- calendar;
- character records;
- routines;
- responsibilities;
- photographs;
- memories;
- future event eligibility.

This connects directly to the living-world calendar and relationship systems.

---

# 11. Death does not erase identity

After death, the character's name remains present in the world's history.

Their name may appear through:
- diaries;
- photographs;
- graves;
- memorials;
- messages;
- conversations;
- family records;
- memories;
- belongings.

The world should not behave as though the person never existed.

---

# 12. Naming and emotional discovery

A player's emotional connection should develop through accumulated knowledge.

A person initially encountered as a stranger can eventually become:
- “someone I know”; 
- “someone I trust”; 
- “someone I love”; 
- “someone I lost.”

The character's name becomes part of that emotional history.

---

# 13. Internal identifiers

Technical systems may still use stable internal IDs for implementation.

For example:

`character_id = char_8f31...`

This is strictly a database identifier.

The player-facing system uses the canonical human identity:

`Name: [canonical name]`

Internal IDs must never be used as substitutes for names in dialogue, cinematic scripts, menus or narrative documents once a character has been canonized.

---

# 14. Naming registry

A master character registry should prevent accidental duplicate names and continuity errors.

The registry tracks:
- canonical name;
- aliases;
- family name relationships;
- age;
- birth events;
- death status where applicable;
- character role;
- visual reference status;
- voice status;
- narrative status.

---

# 15. Production rule for the 40+ cast

The planned large cast should be created as a **named ensemble**, not a numbered list.

Before a major character is treated as production-ready, their identity must be sufficiently defined to answer:

**Who are they?**

**What is their name?**

**Who loves them?**

**Who fears them?**

**Who depends on them?**

**What do they want?**

**What are they hiding?**

**What do they remember?**

**What could they lose?**

---

# 16. Layer 39 acceptance criteria

- [ ] Every meaningful character has a canonical name.
- [ ] Newborn characters receive names.
- [ ] Internal IDs remain technical only.
- [ ] Character identity records are supported.
- [ ] Names reflect believable personal/cultural context.
- [ ] Nicknames and private forms of address are supported.
- [ ] Public/private identities can coexist where narratively justified.
- [ ] Characters remain recognizable through multiple identity signals.
- [ ] Children have age-appropriate identity continuity.
- [ ] Birth creates a named world entity.
- [ ] Death preserves a character's identity in world history.
- [ ] Names can become emotionally meaningful through accumulated play.
- [ ] A master naming registry prevents duplication and continuity errors.
- [ ] The 40+ character ensemble is treated as a named human community rather than numbered game pieces.

## Direction lock
**The player will never be asked to care about “Character 014.” They will meet a person with a name, a face, a voice, a history and people who know them. Even the newborn will have a name.**
