# Chapter 2 — Layer 41: Character Generation & Cast Bible

## Status
**Creative direction locked.**

## Purpose
Establish the creative foundation for turning the RIDE OR DIE cast into production-ready people without prematurely assigning identities that have not been creatively approved.

Layer 41 defines the character-development standard. The current canonical 69-entry roster and detailed visual appearance specifications are maintained separately in the master cast registry and `PRODUCTION/SEASON-1/CHARACTER-VISUAL-MASTER.md`.

---

# 1. Core principle

> **Create people first. Create archetypes last.**

Characters may begin from a compelling premise, but they must develop into contradictory, emotionally believable individuals with their own histories, relationships and agency.

---

# 2. Character creation sequence

Every major character follows this sequence:

1. premise;
2. identity;
3. personal history;
4. relationships;
5. wants and fears;
6. contradictions;
7. daily life;
8. secrets;
9. visual identity;
10. voice/performance identity;
11. event participation;
12. world-state behaviour;
13. arc;
14. continuity review;
15. canon approval.

---

# 3. Canonical name requirement

A character does not become production-canonical until they have a human name, except for deliberately non-human entities such as ECHO, which retain their own canonical identity rules.

Temporary internal IDs may be used by technical systems, but creative documents should use the character's working name once established.

No player-facing numbered characters.

The player's customizable Protagonist is a special player-character slot: it has a stable role and ID but no single project-wide face, body or personal visual identity. The player's selected appearance becomes canonical for that playthrough only.

---

# 4. Character premise

Each character begins with a concise premise explaining why their life belongs in the world.

A premise may involve:
- family;
- work;
- ambition;
- love;
- grief;
- responsibility;
- secrecy;
- social reputation;
- faith;
- parenthood;
- artistic expression;
- survival;
- moral conflict.

The premise is a starting point, not the character's entire personality.

---

# 5. Human contradiction

Core characters should contain meaningful contradictions.

Examples:
- generous but controlling;
- brave in public but frightened privately;
- admired professionally but lonely personally;
- outwardly cruel but deeply protective of one person;
- innocent in one area of life and compromised in another.

Contradiction must arise from history rather than being added as a gimmick.

---

# 6. Moral complexity

Characters are not assigned permanent “good” or “evil” labels.

The game can depict:
- selfish choices;
- impulsive decisions;
- betrayal;
- remorse;
- protection;
- manipulation;
- courage;
- cowardice;
- forgiveness;
- denial.

The narrative records consequences rather than rewarding or condemning every action with a simplistic morality meter.

---

# 7. Relationship-first design

A character should be understood partly through the people around them.

Each core character receives a relationship map containing:
- family;
- friends;
- romantic relationships;
- rivals;
- coworkers;
- dependents;
- trusted people;
- people they fear;
- people who misunderstand them.

Relationships can conflict with one another.

---

# 8. Private versus public self

Where appropriate, characters have different social presentations.

Examples:
- celebrity versus private individual;
- boss versus parent;
- religious public identity versus private uncertainty;
- admired spouse versus hidden betrayal;
- feared person versus vulnerable caregiver.

The difference must emerge through scenes rather than exposition dumps.

---

# 9. Daily life test

For every major character, the design team should be able to answer:

**What were they doing yesterday before the player met them?**

**What will they do tomorrow if the player never intervenes?**

**What is in their home?**

**Who calls them?**

**What do they worry about at night?**

**What ordinary task do they hate?**

This prevents characters from existing only when the protagonist arrives.

---

# 10. Personal objects

Each major character receives several identity anchors.

Examples:
- photograph;
- jewellery;
- recipe book;
- instrument;
- child's toy;
- diary;
- work object;
- inherited item;
- phone content;
- clothing item.

Objects can become evidence, memories or relationship symbols.

---

# 11. Voice and performance

Character voices should reflect:
- age;
- personality;
- emotional state;
- social context;
- relationships;
- environment.

Performance should include silence, hesitation, breathing, interruptions and imperfect emotional control.

Not every line should sound polished.

---

# 12. Visual identity

Character visuals must communicate identity without relying on exposition.

The authoritative visual specification for the current cast is `PRODUCTION/SEASON-1/CHARACTER-VISUAL-MASTER.md`. That master must define or reference, as applicable:
- canonical age or player-selected age;
- silhouette;
- posture;
- facial structure;
- skin and material detail;
- hair;
- clothing choices;
- accessories;
- movement;
- facial expression;
- signs of occupation;
- signs of family life;
- recognition anchors;
- pre-Collapse and Collapse wardrobe evolution.

Visual details must remain consistent with the character's circumstances.

---

# 13. Character-specific wardrobe

Major characters should have context-specific clothing rather than one permanent outfit.

Possible contexts:
- work;
- home;
- school;
- ceremony;
- wedding;
- birthday;
- night out;
- travel;
- weather changes;
- aftermath of major events.

Wardrobe continuity can communicate time passage and life changes.

---

# 14. Life-stage realism

Characters at different life stages require different behavioural and visual design.

This includes:
- newborns;
- children;
- teenagers;
- young adults;
- adults;
- older adults;
- elders.

A newborn is not a miniature adult NPC. Their behaviour, mobility and interactions must be developmentally appropriate.

Fixed-age characters must visually read as their canonical age. Do not use exaggerated wrinkles or artificial youthfulness as shorthand. The Protagonist follows the same age-aware rules using the player's selected age.

---

# 15. Character agency test

For each core character, define at least several things they can initiate without the player.

Examples:
- call someone;
- leave home;
- attend an event;
- confront another character;
- hide evidence;
- help someone;
- change plans;
- make a purchase;
- visit family;
- disappear temporarily.

The protagonist is not the center of every person's universe.

---

# 16. Knowledge profile

Every major character receives a knowledge profile:

- known facts;
- suspicions;
- false beliefs;
- secrets;
- people they trust;
- information they are seeking.

This profile changes as the world evolves.

---

# 17. Emotional profile

Characters have emotional baselines and triggers.

Examples:
- abandonment;
- betrayal;
- public humiliation;
- fear for a child;
- grief;
- recognition;
- affection;
- guilt.

Triggers should influence behaviour without producing repetitive scripted reactions.

---

# 18. Consequence profile

Each core character should have several meaningful consequence paths.

A choice can affect:
- relationship;
- employment;
- reputation;
- family;
- location;
- event attendance;
- trust;
- future knowledge;
- survival.

Not every consequence needs to be visible immediately.

---

# 19. Death and disappearance planning

For characters whose stories may end through death or disappearance, define:
- who notices first;
- who looks for them;
- who benefits;
- who grieves;
- who becomes suspicious;
- what happens to their belongings;
- what information dies with them;
- what evidence remains.

The objective is narrative continuity, not shock for its own sake.

---

# 20. Character introduction styles

Characters can be introduced through:
- direct conversation;
- overheard dialogue;
- work;
- family activity;
- a wedding;
- a birthday;
- a photograph;
- a diary;
- a public performance;
- an unexpected encounter.

The player should often learn who someone is by observing them rather than receiving a biography card.

---

# 21. Cast-bible categories

The established ensemble deliberately covers multiple narrative functions without becoming rigid archetypes.

Suggested creative buckets:
- families;
- friends;
- lovers;
- rivals;
- parents;
- children;
- professionals;
- artists;
- public figures;
- religious/community figures;
- workers;
- students;
- hidden antagonistic networks;
- vulnerable people;
- morally ambiguous people.

One character may belong to several categories.

---

# 22. Celebrity characters

The two established celebrity concepts are included in the cast bible.

Their opposing public reputations should be preserved:

### Celebrity A
Publicly adored; privately involved in a secret ritualistic society and manipulating access to potential victims/sacrifices.

### Celebrity B
Publicly disliked for confronting uncomfortable truths through music; privately helps people without seeking recognition.

Neither character should be reduced to “celebrity villain” or “celebrity hero.” Their ordinary lives and contradictions must remain visible.

---

# 23. Family and parenthood characters

The established family concepts remain part of the cast architecture, including:
- the stay-at-home mother facing marital betrayal;
- the teen mother whose pregnancy and birth occur within the story timeline;
- the newborn child created through that birth event.

Their stories must retain individual identities beyond their relationships.

---

# 24. Sensitive identity rule

Characters may have identities involving sexuality, religion, family structure, class, profession or personal history.

These identities should be treated as dimensions of a whole person rather than used as shorthand for morality.

A character can be queer, religious, wealthy, poor, admired, disliked, compassionate, harmful or conflicted without those categories determining whether they are “good.”

---

# 25. Cast growth

The current canonical roster is 51 main entries plus 18 supporting entries, for 69 total. New named characters may be added only when the canon is explicitly expanded; the existing 69 must not be replaced or silently redefined.

A character can begin as a minor community member and become important through emergent play.

---

# 26. Canon approval checklist

Before final approval, ask:

- Do they have a name or an intentionally defined non-human identity?
- Can I describe their life without mentioning the protagonist?
- Do they have someone they love?
- Do they have someone they fear or distrust?
- Do they have something they want?
- Do they have something they regret?
- Do they have a contradiction?
- Do they have a recognizable visual identity?
- Do they have meaningful possessions?
- Do they have a plausible routine?
- Do they know some things but not others?
- Can they act without the player?
- Would their absence change something?
- Is their appearance consistent with the canonical visual master?
- Is their age presentation accurate and consistent?

If most answers are no, the character is not ready.

---

# 27. Layer 41 acceptance criteria

- [ ] A formal character creation sequence exists.
- [ ] Characters receive names before production canonization, except deliberately non-human identities such as ECHO.
- [ ] Human contradiction is required for core characters.
- [ ] Moral complexity replaces simplistic morality labels.
- [ ] Relationship maps are part of every core character dossier.
- [ ] Public/private identity differences can be represented.
- [ ] Every core character passes a daily-life test.
- [ ] Personal objects can anchor character identity.
- [ ] Voice and physical performance are part of character design.
- [ ] Visual identity includes wardrobe and movement.
- [ ] Life-stage behaviour is developmentally appropriate.
- [ ] Canonical age or player-selected age is respected.
- [ ] Characters can initiate actions independently.
- [ ] Knowledge boundaries are tracked.
- [ ] Emotional triggers and baselines are tracked.
- [ ] Consequence paths are designed before production.
- [ ] Death/disappearance continuity is planned where applicable.
- [ ] Characters can be introduced naturally through the world.
- [ ] The established ensemble covers diverse life roles without rigid archetypes.
- [ ] The two celebrity concepts remain part of the same cast system.
- [ ] Established family and parenthood concepts remain canon.
- [ ] Sensitive identities are treated as dimensions of whole characters.
- [ ] The 69-entry roster is preserved unless canon explicitly changes it.
- [ ] A character canon approval checklist exists.

## Direction lock
**The next step is not to manufacture generic faces. It is to create believable lives. The canonical visual master then turns those approved lives into consistent faces, bodies, wardrobes, voices and game-ready assets.**
