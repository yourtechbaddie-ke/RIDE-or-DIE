# Chapter 3 — Layer 45: Survival Loop & Player Agency

## Status
**Canon direction locked.**

## Purpose
Define the playable survival loop that turns RIDE OR DIE from a narrative world into a game. The player must continuously make meaningful decisions while physically navigating a changing world.

## 1. Core philosophy

> **The player survives through decisions, preparation, observation and adaptation—not through grinding.**

RIDE OR DIE is not primarily an XP progression game. Character development, resources, relationships and knowledge are consequences of play.

## 2. Core gameplay loop

**Explore → Observe → Assess → Prepare → Act → Survive / Fail → Recover → Adapt → Discover → Continue.**

Every stage should create opportunities for player agency.

## 3. Exploration

Players physically navigate environments to discover:
- resources;
- people;
- shelter;
- routes;
- evidence;
- hazards;
- environmental changes;
- hidden spaces;
- personal objects;
- signs of previous survivors.

Exploration should reward attention rather than only waypoint completion.

## 4. Observation

The player can inspect the environment before committing to a decision.

Useful observations may include:
- movement behind windows;
- unusual sounds;
- damaged structures;
- footprints;
- abandoned vehicles;
- food left behind;
- lights in distant buildings;
- photographs;
- diary pages;
- changes in weather;
- NPC behaviour.

## 5. Assessment

The player weighs:
- immediate danger;
- available resources;
- companion condition;
- time;
- route safety;
- potential rewards;
- unknown information;
- consequences of failure.

The game should provide enough information for informed decisions without revealing everything.

## 6. Preparation

Players can prepare before undertaking risky actions.

Potential preparation includes:
- packing supplies;
- treating injuries;
- preparing food;
- repairing equipment;
- choosing companions;
- planning routes;
- securing shelter;
- conserving power;
- deciding what to leave behind.

Preparation itself should consume time and resources.

## 7. Action

Actions are physically performed where practical.

Examples:
- opening and searching containers;
- cooking;
- climbing;
- driving;
- moving furniture;
- hiding;
- treating wounds;
- carrying someone;
- repairing equipment;
- navigating;
- communicating;
- escaping.

## 8. Survival outcomes

An action can produce:
- success;
- partial success;
- injury;
- resource loss;
- relationship consequences;
- discovery;
- delayed consequences;
- death;
- a new opportunity.

Failure should usually change the situation rather than simply display a game-over screen.

## 9. Resource philosophy

Resources are meaningful because they connect directly to decisions.

Potential categories:
- food;
- water;
- medicine;
- fuel;
- batteries;
- tools;
- clothing;
- shelter materials;
- information;
- transportation.

The game should avoid excessive inventory clutter. Items should matter.

## 10. Knowledge as a resource

Information is itself a survival resource.

Knowing:
- which road is blocked;
- who can be trusted;
- where medicine exists;
- when a threat appears;
- who controls a location;
- what happened to a missing person;
- what Echo Aurora may signify;
can be as valuable as physical supplies.

## 11. Relationships as gameplay

Relationships can affect survival.

A trusted companion may:
- warn the player;
- share resources;
- assist with tasks;
- provide medical or technical knowledge;
- protect someone;
- reveal information.

A damaged relationship may produce:
- refusal to help;
- withholding information;
- abandonment;
- betrayal;
- conflict.

Relationships should never be reduced to a simple numeric affection meter.

## 12. Companion agency

Companions should have their own needs and opinions.

They can:
- disagree with the player;
- make suggestions;
- refuse dangerous plans;
- act independently;
- become frightened;
- protect someone else;
- leave;
- return;
- become injured;
- die.

The player should not have absolute control over every other person.

## 13. Hide-and-hunt system

Some encounters are designed around evasion.

Players may need to:
- choose hiding places;
- control noise;
- monitor threat movement;
- wait for openings;
- redirect attention;
- protect companions while concealed;
- escape without being detected.

Combat is not required for survival.

## 14. Consequential choices

Choices should change one or more of:
- resources;
- relationships;
- information;
- world state;
- character survival;
- future access;
- location safety;
- later events.

The game should avoid fake choices that always converge immediately.

## 15. Delayed consequences

Some decisions should pay off much later.

Examples:
- helping a stranger who later becomes an ally;
- taking an item that someone else needed;
- revealing a secret;
- leaving a person behind;
- saving a resource for a future emergency;
- destroying evidence;
- choosing one route over another.

The player may not immediately understand the significance of a choice.

## 16. Survival without omniscience

The player should never have complete information.

This creates uncertainty without relying on arbitrary randomness.

The player can make the best decision they can based on what their character actually knows.

## 17. Character capability

Different characters may approach the same survival problem differently.

Skills can influence:
- available actions;
- efficiency;
- risk;
- speed;
- information gained;
- recovery.

Skills should support character identity rather than creating rigid RPG classes.

## 18. Emotional cost

A mechanically successful decision can still be emotionally devastating.

Example:

The player reaches a safe vehicle but cannot fit everyone inside.

There is no mathematically perfect solution.

The game records who was left behind and allows the consequences to persist.

## 19. Death and continuation

Death should not always mean the end of the entire game.

Depending on the mode and narrative structure, the player may continue through:
- another surviving character;
- a family member;
- a successor character;
- a new generation;
- an alternate route.

This supports the established generational universe.

## 20. Tariq and survival agency

When Tariq is a newborn, his survival needs create constraints for adults.

As he grows, his capabilities can gradually increase.

Eventually, as a teenager, he can make meaningful decisions of his own.

He should never become an unrealistic combat substitute simply because he is older.

## 21. World persistence

Actions can alter physical spaces.

Examples:
- barricading a door;
- taking supplies;
- damaging a vehicle;
- moving furniture;
- leaving evidence;
- establishing a shelter;
- changing a relationship;
- creating a memorial.

Returning to the same place should sometimes reveal the player's history there.

## 22. No forced heroism

The player is not required to save everyone.

Survival can involve:
- selfish decisions;
- altruistic decisions;
- compromises;
- mistakes;
- calculated risks;
- sacrifices.

The game should judge consequences through the world rather than constantly judging the player through morality pop-ups.

## 23. Core player fantasy

The player should feel:

> **“I am trying to keep myself and the people I care about alive in a world I cannot completely understand.”**

That feeling is more important than conventional progression mechanics.

## 24. Canonical design statement

> **RIDE OR DIE gives the player agency without giving them certainty. Survival is a chain of decisions whose consequences become part of the world's history.**

## Acceptance criteria

- [ ] Physical exploration is central.
- [ ] Observation affects decisions.
- [ ] Preparation consumes meaningful resources/time.
- [ ] Actions are playable rather than purely textual.
- [ ] Failure can create persistent consequences.
- [ ] Resources remain meaningful and limited.
- [ ] Knowledge functions as a survival resource.
- [ ] Companions have independent agency.
- [ ] Hide-and-hunt gameplay is supported.
- [ ] Choices can have delayed consequences.
- [ ] Characters have differentiated capabilities.
- [ ] Emotional costs can accompany successful survival decisions.
- [ ] Generational continuation can follow character death.
- [ ] The world can remember player actions.
- [ ] The game does not force the player into constant heroism.

## Direction lock
**Survival is not a scoreboard. It is what happens when every decision starts to matter.**
